Children's Day falls on 14 November, Jawaharlal Nehru's birthday, and in most Indian schools that means a day of games and a small gift on the way out the door. Offices with a CSR calendar or a crèche on site tend to do some version of the same thing. Nobody plans the gift far in advance. It's usually whatever's stacked near the billing counter at a stationery shop, picked the week before.

This year there's an unusual backdrop to that last-minute pick, and it has nothing to do with gifting at all.

## India's toy industry just had its best year on paper

A Press Information Bureau update, reported by Drishti IAS in [July 2026](https://www.drishtiias.com/daily-updates/daily-news-analysis/strengthening-indias-toy-ecosystem), laid out the numbers. India's toy exports rose from $152.7 million in 2017-18 to $384.7 million in 2025-26, a jump of more than 151%. The trade balance flipped too, from a $213 million deficit in 2017-18 to a $152 million surplus last year. Employment in the sector more than doubled over roughly the same stretch, from 8,685 workers in 2018-19 to 17,693 in 2023-24.

Most of that turnaround gets credited to a rule most parents have never heard named: the Toys Quality Control Order, which made BIS certification compulsory for every toy sold in India, imported or domestic, from January 2021. By May 2026, BIS had issued 1,786 licences to Indian manufacturers and 56 to foreign ones.

That's a real regulatory success story, worth knowing before you're standing in a toy aisle. But read what the certification actually checks: sharp edges, small parts, flammability, how much lead or phthalate can leach out of the paint. Safety and material are different questions. Only one of them has a government order behind it right now, so a fully certified, perfectly safe toy can still be built entirely from virgin plastic with nowhere to go once a child outgrows it.

## A different kind of return gift

That's where a plantable or craft-based gift sits a little differently: it was never answering the same question in the first place, not a matter of dodging any rule. A [Grow Me Kit](/shop/grow-me-kit/) (₹299) is a pot, a pre-mixed soil blend and a seed packet in one box. A child opens it, plants it, and checks on it for a week or two, and the kit's job is basically done the moment something sprouts. Our [seed pencils](/shop/seed-pencils-set-of-4-boxes/) write like an ordinary pencil until they're too short to hold, then get planted instead of binned. [Seed balls](/shop/seed-balls-set-of-10/) work the same way: plant them when you're ready, rather than leaving them in a drawer. The [DIY animal keychain kit](/shop/diy-animal-keychain-kit/) skips planting altogether, a child paints their own wooden animal cutout and clips it onto a bag or keys instead.

None of these are marketed as toys in the first place, so this isn't a claim about certification either way. It's simpler: a seed pencil or a Grow Me Kit has somewhere to go after the gifting moment ends, and a plastic action figure usually doesn't.

For kids who'd rather colour than plant, [The Happy Mandala Book](/shop/mandala-book/) is twenty designs on recycled paper at ₹199. We don't bundle colour pencils in any more, so it leans on whatever's already in the pencil case at home. Smaller gift, same classroom return-gift budget.
