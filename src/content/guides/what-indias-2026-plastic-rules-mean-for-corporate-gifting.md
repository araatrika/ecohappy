Diwali corporate gifting usually starts the same way: a WhatsApp message to 4 or 5 vendors asking for hamper quotes, a scramble over headcount and budget, and a box that says "eco-friendly" somewhere on the label. This year there's a new variable in that conversation, and it has nothing to do with price. On 31 March 2026, the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change notified the Plastic Waste Management (Amendment) Rules, 2026. Diwali, on 8 November, is the first major festive gifting season since.

## What actually changed

The amendment raises how much recycled material plastic packaging has to contain, on an escalating schedule. Rigid plastic packaging needs 30% recycled content this year, climbing to 60% by 2028-29. Flexible plastic packaging moves from 10% to 20% over the same stretch, multi-layered packaging from 5% to 10%. Reuse targets for large plastic water containers climb toward 85% by 2028-29. None of this is written with gift boxes in mind specifically. It targets the producers, importers and brand owners who put plastic packaging into the market, and this year it also reaches the companies that sell them the raw resin and pellets in the first place.

The bigger change is about who checks the paperwork. Compliance used to run mostly on self-declaration through a Central Pollution Control Board portal. Under this amendment, verification shifts to independent "Registered Environment Auditors," a role created under the Environment Audit Rules, 2025. The CPCB had six months from the March notification to publish detailed guidelines for how that auditing actually works. That window closes this month.

## Why self-reporting didn't survive

There's a reason for the shift. In 2023, the CPCB disclosed that plastic recyclers had generated roughly 700,000 fake compliance certificates, about 38 times what those recyclers could actually process, according to a 2024 report from the Centre for Science and Environment. A system built on trust had a trust problem, and third-party audits are the government's answer to it. They arrive right as this year's corporate gifting orders go out the door.

## What this means if the gift was never plastic to begin with

A hamper boxed in a laminated shell with a plastic viewing window and a layer of shrink film sits inside this tightening compliance chain, whatever the sticker on the outside claims. A seed pencil rolled from recycled newspaper and packed in a kraft sleeve was never part of that conversation, because there's no plastic packaging to report on in the first place. Same for a hamper packed in cloth and seed paper. That's true of what we make in our own workshop in Pune: the packaging on our seed pencils and hampers is paper and cloth, not plastic, by design rather than by claim.

This sits next to a rule that's been in force a while longer: the Central Consumer Protection Authority's guidelines against greenwashing, issued in October 2024, which require any claim like "eco-friendly" or "sustainable" to be backed by evidence rather than printed on a box. Between the two rules, a vendor calling a hamper eco-friendly now has two separate reasons to be able to back that up on paper, not just on the packaging.

## What to ask your vendor this year

If you're sourcing gifts this year, ask a narrower question than "is this eco-friendly." Ask what the packaging is actually made of, and if the answer is plastic, ask whether the vendor's EPR registration is something they can show you, not just something they mention. That's a five-minute question over WhatsApp or email, and it tells you more than any label will.
