Diwali this year falls on 8 November. Thirteen days after that, wedding season opens on one of just four widely listed auspicious dates left in November 2026: the 21st, 24th, 25th or 26th. December has more. And this year, that season opens under a rule most couples haven't heard of yet.

## What changed, and when

Maharashtra has had a ban on the sale and use of non-biodegradable plastic flowers since July 2025, but for months it mostly sat on paper. That started to change this year. In February, Chief Minister Devendra Fadnavis told the state legislature that a fresh government resolution was coming to make enforcement "stricter and more effective." By March, environment minister Pankaja Munde had put numbers to it: a first offence draws a ₹5,000 fine, a second draws ₹10,000, and repeat violations can mean three months in jail on top of a ₹25,000 fine. Municipal corporations were given the power to act directly against the markets selling the flowers, and decorators and banquet halls were named specifically as the businesses that would receive notices. Between April 2025 and January 2026 alone, the state says it inspected roughly 125,000 establishments, penalised 3,390 of them, and seized about 67.5 tonnes of single-use plastic under its broader plastic rules.

## What this rule doesn't touch

None of that applies to what you hand your guests on the way out. The ban and its penalties are aimed at decorators, venues and flower markets, specifically artificial flowers used for decor, not at favours or return gifts. We'd rather say that plainly than let the two blur together, because legally they're not the same thing, and nothing currently on the books requires a wedding favour to be made of anything in particular.

But the question the flower ban is really asking out loud, what is this actually made of, and where does it go once the function ends, doesn't stop being fair just because the law has only pointed it at flowers so far. A single plastic favour is a small decision. Four hundred of them, handed out at one wedding, is the same packaging math India's regulators have been working through all year: plastic in corporate gift packaging back in March, now flowers at weddings and festivals. Favours haven't been named yet. That's a gap in the rule, not a reason to stop asking.

## What "sustainable" should actually mean here

"Eco-friendly favours" shows up on enough wedding-planning sites, sitting next to everything from printed trinkets to genuine seed paper, that the phrase barely means anything on its own. The test we'd apply is the same one that applies to any gifting claim: can the vendor name the material, and does the number behind the claim hold up? A favour sold as plantable should sprout when it's planted. A favour sold as recycled paper should come with an actual percentage, not just the word. If a vendor can answer both questions specifically, you're most of the way to an honest choice, whatever the label says.
